# THE NIGHT COUNT — Game Concept

**Final title:** THE NIGHT COUNT
**Tagline:** *The pumps run all night. So does the count.*

---

## One-sentence hook

You work the last six night shifts of a dying desert gas station, where the
company rulebook that tells you never to refuse a customer is not a customer
service policy — it is the only thing keeping you off the count.

## Premise

A filling station on a dead stretch of highway, thirty-one miles from the
nearest town, closing for good in a week. It has hired one night attendant to
work the last six shifts: clean it, stock it, open it at nine, serve whoever
comes in, and lock it at six.

He takes the job for the money and because of **Jacob**, who went missing out
here. Jacob is not the only one — people have gone missing on this stretch,
repeatedly, and it has never been in the paper. That is the fact the player
arrives holding, and it is true.

The station still gets customers. Not many, and they always want something
small. One of them has no car, pays five dollars for nothing, and tells him not
to do five nights.

**He is called Danny, and the game does not tell you that until Night 6** — on
a note from his boss, in the past tense.

## Player fantasy

*"I am the only person awake for thirty miles, I am responsible for this
building, and I am extremely worried about doing my job wrong."*

The fantasy is competence under dread: you are not a soldier or a detective,
you are an employee, and your weapons are a barcode scanner, a cash drawer, a
breaker panel and a mop. The game's power fantasy — such as it is — is being
*good at a boring job* while something patient tests whether you are.

## Horror subgenre

Slow-burn occupational horror / analogue-era rural weird. Sub-flavours:
surveillance horror (the camera bank), social horror (customers), and
procedural dread (rules that must be obeyed exactly).

## Target experience

- Minutes 0–20: mild work anxiety. Learning the register. Nothing is wrong.
- Minutes 20–50: contradiction. The player starts checking things twice.
- Minutes 50–80: rule-fear. The player now believes the rulebook and follows it
  even when it feels insane.
- Minutes 80–110: inversion. The rulebook is *why* the trap works, and the
  player must break exactly one rule, exactly once, at the right target.

Expected full playtime: **90–120 minutes** for a first complete run,
~60 minutes for a repeat run that skips optional discovery.

## Design pillars

1. **The job is the game.** Every horror beat is expressed through a work
   system: ringing up, restocking, mopping, breakers, cameras, logs. There is
   no separate "horror mode" where the work stops.
2. **The rules are real and they are load-bearing.** The manager's binder is
   both onboarding documentation and a survival manual. Everything the threat
   does obeys the rules the binder describes. No arbitrary deaths.
3. **Cross-checking is the core verb.** Window vs. monitor. Log vs. receipt.
   Manifest vs. shelf. Voice on the phone vs. the voice you heard an hour ago.
   The player is always comparing two sources of truth.
4. **Quiet is the default.** Loud things are rare and expensive. The station's
   ambience is dense and continuous; the scariest audio event in the game is a
   *subtraction* from that ambience, not an addition.
5. **Everything connects to Mile 41.** No free-floating spookiness. Every
   anomaly resolves to the 1972 tanker fire, the road, Meridian's policy, or
   Danny.

## What makes this distinct

Most gas-station horror games are "do chores until a monster chases you." Here
the chores *are* the defence, and the monster's method of killing you is to
politely queue up and wait to be served. The central mechanical joke — that
five nights of training you to *never refuse a customer* exists so that the
sixth night can require you to refuse exactly one — is the whole design in
miniature. The threat never touches a door handle. It gets you to complete a
transaction.

The second distinction is the cross-check verb. Rather than a flashlight and a
hiding spot, your tools are a monitor bank and a window, and the game is
constantly asking *which of these two things do you believe.*

## Emotional journey

| Phase | Player feels |
|---|---|
| Night 1 | Slightly bored, slightly nervous about screwing up the register |
| Night 2 | "That was odd." Starts watching the monitors voluntarily |
| Night 3 | Protective of the building; annoyed then frightened by the power |
| Night 4 | Watched. Personally known. Stops trusting the cameras |
| Night 5 | Complicit. Realises Meridian knew, and that she was expected |
| Night 6 | Professional. Grimly, precisely doing the job to get her brother out |
| Ending | Quiet grief, and the specific relief of having closed out correctly |

## Scope boundaries

**In scope:** 6 nights; one station interior + forecourt + back lot; 8 named
customer entities; 1 threat with 3 behavioural stages; 9 work systems; 6 camera
feeds; 3 endings; ~40 readable documents; full menu/settings/save.

**Out of scope (deliberately):** driving sections, combat, inventory crafting,
open world, multiplayer, procedural level generation, more than one building
interior, a spoken-dialogue voice cast (see `VOICE_ASSET_MANIFEST.md` for the
sourcing constraint and what is used instead).
